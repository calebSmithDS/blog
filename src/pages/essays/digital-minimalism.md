## The Addictive Nature of the Attention Economy

The first step in understanding the importance of having an intentional approach to technology, is understanding the nature of the landscape in which that approach is aimed to protect against. Well known social behavioural phycologist Dr Adam Alter when studying digital habits, found that many behaviours shared similar obsessive characteristics to that of substance addiction. Psychological researchers have since published extensive studies demonstrating that digital behavioral addictions share the exact same neural pathways and obsessive characteristics as substance abuse. But what makes this so scary is not that this is an accidental by product, but rather an intentional feature that has been deployed into many digital technologies.  

As many digital technologies such as social media need to make money from their services, they employ advertising as a form of revenue. Companies will pay digital media companies to promote their brands, and these media companies charge them on a per-view basis. Which seems reasonable—the more views an ad gets, generally the more people will buy their product. This economical model allows their services to remain free to their consumers, but everything in life comes with a cost. That's because this model brings with it a tension: social media companies operate based on how much of people's attention they can attain and for how long. This means they are heavily incentivized to try and steal as much of your attention as possible. The more attention, the more money. In fact, when Facebook was initially being developed, it was stated that their objective was: 

> “How do we consume as much of your time and conscious attention as possible?”

With a company like Facebook and its capital, it employs some of the smartest psychologists and engineers in the world specifically to make their services as addictive as possible. 

While many addictions are generally viewed by society as immoral—such as the negative feelings many of us have toward the action of sitting at a pokies machine night after night, thinking that these people have become a slave to their senses and lost self-control—many remain unaware of the silent evil to which they succumb everyday. Tristan Harris noted that in reality, our phones function very similarly to slot machines in our pockets. They both use clever psychological tricks to keep our attention. Attention engineers use many methods, but there are two main psychological principles which they leverage.

Firstly, our brain's natural desire for intermittent positive reinforcement. It was this concept that led to the creation of the like button for Facebook, where it was said that receiving a like would be “a little dopamine hit”. The fact that *if* or *when* you get a like remains unpredictable makes these accumulated dopamine hits significantly larger—as positive rewards that come at random intervals release more dopamine. Dopamine is the brain's feel-good chemical, and the more you get it, the more you desire it, feeding a cycle of dopamine hunting. 

Secondly, our desire for social approval. It's a God-given desire to want to belong, feel respected, and validated, but these companies hack this desire and give us a shallow imitation. We receive a like, a comment, someone's post, and we feel involved or loved. This incentivizes humans to divert their cognitive energy to this quick form of validation. And as mentioned above, you don't know how or when people will interact, putting you in an anxious state of expectation for approval and its satisfying dopamine hit. 

These two principles highlight that many digital technologies use very smart tricks to steal our attention. 

## Existentialism and Your Phone

You may be thinking that these two concepts seem strange together. But if that's the case, that further speaks to the existential crisis that we face. If you reflect on the concept of time, no matter your worldview, it is one of your most valuable things. And yet we walk around everyday with an attention-stealing, time-stealing, distraction device in our pockets. I know people who have averaged 8 hours per day of screen time. 

An argument that is often raised is that it's beneficial: *I learn things, I can keep up to date, or I'm relaxing.* But at what cost? In all the previous instances, your phone is almost always a poor substitute compared to another strategy. Being constantly exposed to that environment makes us crave it, want it, and make up any excuse to use it, if we don't just subconsciously succumb to our habits beforehand. These devices distract us from what matters most. I've heard stories of mothers with their newborns on their phones, rather than enjoying the task of bathing their child. Or going on their phone in social settings, completely negating any benefit social media plays to our connections. These devices, if we let them, can distract us from our purpose. 

## The Digital Minimalism Philosophy

With the stage set, you can see how important it is to combat these distractions. You can't expect mere good intentions to overcome the sheer force of attention engineers preying on all your basic desires. But this is not an anti-technology article; in fact, I'm a software dev who built this very website which you are reading this on. 

Technology is a tool. People forget this. They tend to adopt a maximalist mindset when it comes to digital technologies. They feel they need every new flashy app. They feel if they are not on a platform that they will miss something. But these technologies are meant to serve a purpose. Social media is meant to enable communication, not distract you from your day, ruin your attention span, and limit your ability to function in normal social settings. 

Digital minimalism is this: it's stripping back all the fluff to figure out which digital tools you really need. And how to use them so they only serve that need, and nothing more, protecting you from all the attention tricks and gimmicks. It helps you not feel like you are missing out on tech, but rather that you can say you've never felt more fulfilled with how you use it. Not how it uses you.
